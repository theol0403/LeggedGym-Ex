\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ====================================================================

\subsection{Summary}

We presented a pipeline for vision-based quadruped parkour that uses
monocular depth estimation as an intermediate representation.  Our
systematic experiments revealed that depth signal quality---governed
by model scale and terrain texture---is the primary bottleneck for
student performance, not network architecture.  The best DA2-based
student achieves 87\% gap success, within 7~pp of the ground-truth
depth upper bound.

The domain-shift experiments in Section~\ref{sec:domain} support a
more specific claim.  DA2 is robust to lighting and color variation,
and some unseen textured surfaces transfer well, but not all texture
changes are equally benign.  The clearest failure case is loss of
visible surface structure.  Taken together, these results support
monocular depth as a useful interface between vision and locomotion,
while showing that deployment still depends on the quality of the
recovered geometry.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study is still limited to simulation, so the central open
question is whether the observed robustness transfers to a real
robot.  The current pipeline is also sensitive to weak visual
structure and relies on a relatively large depth model, which may be
costly for onboard deployment.  These constraints make the results a
proof of concept rather than a complete sim-to-real solution.

\subsection{Future Work}

The most important next step is real-world evaluation on the Unitree
Go2.  A second priority is reducing perception cost through smaller
or distilled depth models, or through temporal models that stabilize
predictions across frames.  Longer term, jointly adapting the
perception stack and student policy may further close the remaining
gap to the GT-depth baseline.
